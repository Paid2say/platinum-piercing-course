Bloodborne Pathogens

A bloodborne pathogen is any infectious microorganism carried in human blood that can cause disease when transmitted to another person. In piercing, you work with needles, blood, and open wounds every single day. This is not theoretical — it is your daily exposure risk.

The Big Three

HIV — Human Immunodeficiency Virus

Attacks and destroys the immune system over time. Transmitted through blood, sexual fluids, and breast milk. Not airborne. Survives outside the body only minutes to hours — but that's long enough on a contaminated surface. No cure, but highly manageable with antiretrovirals. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) exists and must be started within 72 hours of exposure.

Hepatitis B (HBV)

Attacks the liver and can cause cirrhosis, liver failure, and liver cancer. Far more infectious than HIV — survives on dry surfaces up to 7 days. Transmitted through blood and sexual fluids. A vaccine exists — every working piercer should be vaccinated. In an unvaccinated person, a single needlestick carries roughly a 6–30% transmission risk.

Hepatitis C (HCV)

Also liver-targeting and the leading cause of liver transplants in the US. No vaccine. Survives on surfaces up to 3 weeks in some conditions. Transmitted primarily through blood-to-blood contact. Needlesticks are the classic transmission route. Now curable with antivirals — but prevention is always the goal.

Standard Precautions

The Rule
Treat every client as if they are carrying a bloodborne pathogen — because you don't always know, and they may not know either.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

  • Gloves: Nitrile preferred. Change between every client. Change if torn mid-procedure.
  • Eye protection: Any splash risk — surface piercings, high-vascular areas — wear it.
  • Mask: Protects both piercer and client from respiratory transmission.

Needle Safety

  • One needle · one client · one time. No exceptions.
  • Never recap a needle with two hands.
  • Needles go directly into a puncture-resistant sharps container immediately after use.
  • Sharps containers are sealed when ¾ full — never overfill.

Exposure Response Protocol

Emergency — Act Immediately
PEP for HIV must begin within 72 hours. Sooner is always better. Seek medical attention the same day as the exposure.
  1. Remove gloves carefully
  2. Wash the site with soap and water for at least 15 minutes
  3. If splash to eyes or mouth — flush with clean water for 15 minutes
  4. Report to your employer immediately
  5. Seek medical attention that day — PEP within 72 hours
  6. Document everything: time, circumstances, source client info if available
  7. Follow-up testing: baseline, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months

First Aid in the Studio

Vasovagal Syncope — Fainting (Most Common Studio Emergency)

Caused by: anxiety, pain, sight of blood, heat, hunger, dehydration.

Warning signs: pale skin, sweating, nausea, lightheadedness, slowed speech.

Prevention: Ask about last meal and water intake. Have clients lie down for sensitive piercings. Talk throughout the procedure.

Response: Lower safely, elevate legs, cool cloth on neck, juice/sugar if conscious. If unconscious beyond 1–2 minutes: call 911.

Allergic Reactions

Local: redness, itching at site — likely metal sensitivity.

Anaphylaxis: spreading hives, throat tightness, difficulty breathing, BP drop — call 911 immediately.

Uncontrolled Bleeding

Direct pressure with sterile gauze. Elevate. Bleeding not slowing in 10 minutes or arterial = call 911.

CPR

Extreme vasovagal response can escalate. Anaphylaxis can stop a heart. A client in your chair is never far from a cardiac emergency.

  • Check scene safety and responsiveness
  • Call 911 — or direct a specific person to call
  • 30 chest compressions to 2 rescue breaths (or compression-only)
  • Use AED as soon as available
  • Continue until EMS arrives
  • Recertify every 2 years — Red Cross or American Heart Association

Epidermis

Outermost layer. Avascular — no blood vessels. 5 sublayers; outermost (stratum corneum) is dead, keratinized cells. Thickness varies dramatically: eyelid ~0.5mm, palm/sole up to 4mm. Breaching it starts the wound-healing cascade.

Dermis

Contains collagen, elastin, hair follicles, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands. Blood vessels live here — this is where piercing bleeds. Nerves live here — this is where the client feels pain. Most surface and lobe piercings sit largely within or just below the dermis.

Hypodermis / Subcutaneous Tissue

Fat and connective tissue beneath the dermis. Deeper piercings (navel, some surface anchors) pass through or anchor in this layer. More fat = more tissue movement = higher migration risk in some placements.

Critical Point — Cartilage
Cartilage relies on the perichondrium for nutrition and immune response. Infection in cartilage can lead to perichondritis, abscess, or permanent deformity. This is why cartilage infections hit harder than lobe infections.

Blood Vessels & Nerve Supply

🖼️

Key Vascular Danger Zones

  • Tongue: Lingual artery runs lateral — tongue piercings go midline only. Visible lingual vein on underside = mark carefully and avoid.
  • Nostril: Facial artery angular branch supplies the nose — lateral placements that go too deep risk this.
  • Ear: Superficial temporal artery anterior to tragus. Posterior auricular artery behind ear. Most ear piercings are low-risk, but placement matters.
  • Genitals: Highly vascularized. Dorsal vein and dorsal artery of penis. Dorsal artery of clitoris runs midline — VCH must respect midline anatomy.

Key Nerves by Site

  • Ear: Vagus nerve branch to the ear stimulated during tragus piercings — can trigger vasovagal response. Warn clients.
  • Tongue: Lingual nerve runs lateral — same risk zone as lingual artery. Too lateral = risk of permanent partial tongue numbness.
  • Nipple: 4th intercostal nerve — deep or misdirected placement can cause permanent sensory changes.
  • Genital: Dorsal nerve of clitoris/penis — injury can cause permanent sensory loss.

Wound Healing Phases

Phase 1 — Hemostasis (0–hours)

Blood vessels constrict; platelets aggregate; clotting cascade activates. Initial crust and dried lymph clients see is from this phase — it is normal. Not infection. Teach this clearly or clients panic.

Phase 2 — Inflammatory (Days 1–5, can extend)

Vasodilation increases blood flow. White blood cells arrive to fight pathogens and clean debris. Signs: redness, warmth, swelling, pain — this is normal and expected. Inflammation ≠ infection. Infection = pathogen has colonized the tissue and is winning.

Phase 3 — Proliferative / Granulation (Weeks–Months)

New collagen laid down. New blood vessels form. Epithelial cells migrate — the fistula (piercing channel) forms here. Downsizing happens during this phase. Too early = trauma. Too late = lever arm causing migration.

Phase 4 — Remodeling / Maturation (Months–Years)

Type III collagen replaced by stronger Type I. Fistula matures and strengthens. Full tensile strength can take up to 2 years. "Healed" means fully remodeled — not just "not actively angry."

Safe Placement & Anatomical Red Flags

What to Assess Before Marking

  • Tissue depth and pinchability: Minimum ~5–8mm for surface placements. Insufficient depth = migration.
  • Skin surface quality: Stretch marks, scar tissue, keloid history, active skin conditions — all change the equation.
  • Vascularity visibility: Visible veins at the proposed site = plan around them. Never pierce through visible vasculature.
  • Movement and friction: Navel with a tight waistband, nipples without a bra during healing — chronic irritation causes complications.
When to Decline
Insufficient tissue · high vascularity at site with no safe path · active infection or lesion · keloid history in high-risk areas · anatomy that structurally prevents safe placement. Declining is professional, not personal.

Core Concept
Aseptic technique is the full collection of practices that prevent pathogenic microorganisms from reaching a wound or sterile field. You can autoclave every tool perfectly and still cause an infection if your aseptic technique breaks down between sterilization and needle entering skin.

Handwashing

🖼️
[ Add Image Here ]
WHO 6-step handwashing technique poster / sink setup photo / demonstration

When Handwashing Is Mandatory

  • Before entering the work area at start of shift
  • Before setting up any piercing tray
  • After touching your face, hair, phone, or clothing
  • Before donning gloves
  • After removing gloves
  • After handling contaminated equipment or waste
  • Before and after any client contact
  • After using the restroom, eating, drinking, or any break

Proper Technique — WHO 6-Step Method (minimum 20 seconds)

  1. Wet hands with clean running water
  2. Apply soap — enough to lather all surfaces
  3. Rub palms together
  4. Rub back of each hand with interlaced fingers
  5. Interlace fingers palms together — rub between fingers
  6. Lock fingers, rub backs of fingers against opposite palms
  7. Rotational rub of each thumb
  8. Rub fingertips and nails against palm of opposite hand
  9. Rinse thoroughly under running water
  10. Dry with single-use paper towel — use towel to turn off faucet
Important
Alcohol-based hand sanitizer is NOT effective against all pathogens (C. diff, norovirus). Soap and water is always the safer default after glove removal or when hands are visibly soiled.

Proper glove doffing (wrist-peel) technique — step by step

Selection

Nitrile only. Latex risks allergic reactions. Vinyl is not appropriate for piercing — insufficient barrier protection. Correct fit matters: too loose = dexterity loss and gaps; too tight = tears easily.

Mandatory Glove Changes

  • Between every client — no exceptions
  • If a glove tears at any point during the procedure
  • After touching anything outside the sterile field
  • After handling contaminated items before returning to a clean task

Doffing — The Step Most Piercers Rush

  1. Pinch the outside of one glove at the wrist — do not touch skin
  2. Peel off turning inside out — contamination contained
  3. Hold removed glove in still-gloved hand
  4. Slide two fingers of bare hand under cuff of remaining glove — inside only
  5. Peel off second glove turning inside out, enclosing first
  6. Dispose in biohazard waste immediately
  7. Wash hands immediately
Common Error
Snapping gloves off at the fingertips aerosolizes surface contamination. Always peel from the wrist down.

Skin Preparation

🖼️
[ Add Image Here ]
Skin prep product lineup (CHG, BZK, povidone-iodine) / concentric circle technique demo

Standard Skin Prep Protocol — In Order

  1. Examine the site. Active lesions, rashes, infections = postpone.
  2. Remove hair if necessary. Single-use disposable razor only. Never shared.
  3. Apply antiseptic:
    • CHG 2–4%: Standard for most sites. Broad-spectrum, persistent. Not for mucous membranes.
    • BZK: For mucous membranes (nostril interior, oral).
    • Povidone-iodine: Stains; less persistent; fully remove before piercing.
  4. Apply outward in concentric circles. Never back toward center.
  5. Allow full dry time. Rushing this is a real infection risk.
  6. Mark placement with sterile skin marker or gentian violet on sterile toothpick.
  7. Do not touch prepped site with non-sterile items. If contaminated, re-prep.

Cross-Contamination Prevention

🖼️
[ Add Image Here ]
Clean vs. contaminated zone tray setup — overhead photo showing proper separation

Clean vs. Contaminated Zones

  • Clean zone: Sterile jewelry, needles, tools. Nothing that has touched the client enters here.
  • Contaminated zone: Used needles, removed jewelry, used gauze, used gloves.
  • These must be physically separated — different trays or different surfaces. Not just "different sides."

What Instantly Contaminates Your Clean Field

  • Touching your face, hair, mask, or clothing with gloved hands
  • Reaching across the client or the tray
  • Checking your phone
  • Picking up a dropped item from the floor
  • Using the same product bottle for multiple clients without barrier protection
  • Uncovered surfaces in the work area
Barrier Precautions
All non-cleanable surfaces get single-use barriers before each client: arm rests, headrests, chair contact points, light handles, tray surfaces. Removed from the outside, discarded after each client. Surface underneath still gets disinfected with EPA-registered disinfectant.

Sharps Handling & Disposal

🖼️
[ Add Image Here ]
Sharps container placement in room / direct disposal demonstration / container seal level marking
The Rule — No Exceptions
Piercing needles are single-use. Never re-used. Never re-capped by reaching. Never set on any surface after use. Needle goes from client to sharps container in one motion.

Sharps Container Requirements

  • Puncture-resistant
  • Leak-proof sides and bottom
  • Wide enough to deposit without touching sides
  • Labeled with biohazard symbol
  • Sealed at ¾ full — never overfill

Disposal

Sharps containers are regulated medical waste in every state. Use dedicated medical waste services, mail-back programs, or pharmacy/health department drop-off. Never dispose in regular trash. Track all disposal for OSHA compliance.

Key Takeaways — Module 3
  • Handwashing is not optional at any mandatory step — gloves do not substitute for clean hands
  • Aseptic technique is a system — one break in the chain compromises the entire procedure
  • Clean and contaminated zones must be physically separated, not just mentally organized
  • Skin prep has a required dry time — rushing it is a real infection risk
  • Sharps go directly from client to container — no surfaces, no pauses, no exceptions
